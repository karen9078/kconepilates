How to Start a Pilates Studio: Equipment, Layout & Budget

Opening a Pilates studio is less about buying the biggest reformer and more about matching the equipment plan to your training format, your room, and the number of clients you can realistically serve. This guide walks through the decisions that matter before you place a factory order.

Quick Answer

1. Choose your studio format first — group, private, hybrid or rehab. The equipment mix follows the format.

2. Build the mix around reformers — a typical start is 4–8 professional reformers, one Cadillac, two to four chairs and a couple of barrels.

3. Count machines by class size — one reformer per client in your largest class, plus roughly one spare per 4–6 in use.

4. Budget the full launch — machines plus export packaging, freight, customs, installation and training. KCONE quotes factory-direct on FOB Shanghai or DDU.

What studio format should you choose first?

The equipment plan follows the format, not the other way around. Before looking at machines, be clear about the model you are building:

  • Group reformer studio: classes of 4–12 clients on reformers, the most common professional format and the easiest to staff per client.
  • Private / semi-private studio: one-on-one and duet sessions, fewer machines but more Cadillac, barrel and chair work, with higher per-session pricing.
  • Hybrid studio: a small group area plus private suites — the fastest-growing model for new owners because it spreads risk.
  • Rehab-oriented studio: closer work with physiotherapists or clinical partners, which shifts the mix toward reformers, trapeze tables and upright training units.

Your format also defines your teaching system. Some owners train with a specific certification body, which can influence the equipment they feel most confident teaching on. Decide this before you start comparing machines, because it will guide every later choice.

What equipment does a new Pilates studio need?

A balanced commercial studio rarely runs on reformers alone. Clients expect a mix, and the mix is what justifies a studio membership rather than a home reformer:

  • Reformers (the core): the machines your group classes run on. Most studios start with 4–8 professional reformers from our factory collection.
  • Cadillac / trapeze table: essential for private work, assisted stretching and advanced repertoire. A full-size Cadillac covers most studio needs.
  • Wunda Chair: a small footprint, high-value machine for balance, strength and private sessions. Our Wunda Chairs — including a natural-wood version — are the most common picks.
  • Ladder Barrel and Spine Corrector: compact tools that add variety to classes and private sessions without taking much floor space.
  • Wall units and upright trainers: our wall tower fits small spaces, and the PowerHouse RX — our upright rehab trainer — supports standing, rehab-style work for an aging or clinical client base.

How many reformers do you actually need?

Reformer count is driven by class size and schedule turnover, not by how many machines fit in the room. A practical way to calculate it:

  • Decide the maximum class size you will teach (for example 6 clients).
  • Plan one reformer per client in that largest class.
  • Add spare capacity for maintenance, spring changes and the occasional machine out of service — most studios plan roughly one spare reformer per 4–6 in use.
  • Estimate turnover: a 50-minute class with 10 minutes of reset time means each reformer is used about once per hour.

A studio running 6-client classes across a 10-hour teaching day therefore needs roughly 6–8 reformers to keep the schedule flowing. Over-buying reformers while under-buying Cadillacs and chairs is the most common mistake new owners make — the mix usually matters more than the reformer count.

How much space does a Pilates studio need?

Equipment is only part of the plan; the room has to work around it. Confirm these points before signing a lease:

  • Reformer spacing: allow roughly 1.2–1.5 m between reformers for safe entry and exit during class, plus clear aisles to the door.
  • Ceiling height: check that Cadillac bars and tower springs have full range of motion — low ceilings rule out some equipment.
  • Floor loading: professional reformers weigh roughly 60–90 kg; confirm the floor can take the load before the machines arrive.
  • Access route: check door widths, corridors and elevators, because large machines move in as packed units.
  • Storage: allocate space for mats, props, spare springs and toolkits so the studio floor stays clean and safe.
  • Power and lighting: plan outlets and lighting around the class layout before installation.

Getting a rough floor plan to your equipment supplier helps them flag spacing and layout issues before you order. KCONE can review a studio floor plan together with your equipment list and quotation.

How much does it cost to equip a Pilates studio?

Professional Pilates equipment prices vary widely depending on frame material, spring quality, upholstery and whether you buy through a reseller or direct from the factory. Instead of a fixed number, understand the cost drivers:

  • Frame material: carbon steel, stainless steel and anodized aluminum differ in price, weight and corrosion resistance.
  • Spring systems: the spring set is the heart of a reformer; higher-grade springs with consistent tension cost more and last longer.
  • Upholstery: commercial-grade foam and vinyl hold up to daily use far better than home-grade materials.
  • Brand vs. factory-direct: buying from a manufacturer such as KCONE removes reseller margins, which is why most new studios and distributors start with factory-direct quotations.

Budget for more than the machines themselves:

  • Export packaging (wooden crate or protective carton) and packed dimensions.
  • International freight from the FOB port with your own forwarder.
  • Destination customs, import duties and local compliance checks.
  • Installation, spare parts and instructor training.

Should you buy from a manufacturer or a reseller?

For a full studio setup, the difference between a reseller and a factory-direct manufacturer shows up in three places: product consistency, packaging and after-sales support.

  • Product consistency: a manufacturer with its own design and production team can support repeat orders, spare parts and future models under the same quality standard.
  • Export packaging: large Pilates machines ship in professional wooden crates or cartons. Confirm the packed dimensions and weight, not just the product dimensions, before arranging freight.
  • After-sales: setup guidance, maintenance advice and spare parts coordination matter more for equipment than for any other purchase in your studio.

How do ordering, packaging and FOB handover work?

Professional equipment is ordered as a project, not a parcel. The ordering sequence that works for most new studios:

  • Send a quotation request with your models, quantities, preferred colors, timeline and FOB port preference (KCONE quotes on FOB Shanghai or DDU).
  • Confirm specifications, included accessories, packaging details and production lead time before order confirmation.
  • Arrange the main international freight with your own forwarder after the port handover.
  • Plan installation — most reformers arrive assembled or semi-assembled, but verify what tools and time your team needs.

How do you plan for training and staffing?

Equipment launches the studio; instructors keep it running. Before opening day:

  • Confirm every instructor has a certification your market recognizes, and that they are comfortable teaching on the specific machines you bought.
  • Consider ongoing training partnerships. KCONE works with PHI Pilates on joint equipment R&D and professional instructor training, so studio owners can combine equipment purchases with instructor development.
  • Hire for the first few months of class load, not the final schedule — many new studios overstaff and under-market.
