Professional Pilates Equipment Buying Guide for Studios

A step-by-step guide for studio owners, distributors and training academies planning professional Pilates equipment: decide the studio format, build the equipment mix, work out machine counts, plan the room, set a budget, and order factory-direct on FOB Shanghai or DDU terms.

Best forNew and expanding studios, distributors, training centers and rehab-oriented facilities planning a professional Pilates equipment order.
Main decisionMatch the equipment mix, machine count and budget to your studio format, floor area, class size and target users.

1. Decide your studio format first

Professional Pilates equipment should be chosen around the way the studio actually operates — not only around appearance. A private training room, a group reformer studio, an instructor academy and a rehabilitation-oriented facility need different combinations of machines, quantities and accessories.

Studio FormatTypical Class SizeEquipment Focus
Group reformer studio4–12 clients per classConsistent reformer configurations first; chairs and barrels as supporting equipment.
Private / semi-private studio1–3 clients per sessionFewer machines, broader mix: reformer, Cadillac, Wunda Chair, barrel.
Hybrid studioGroup classes + private suitesA small reformer group area plus a dedicated private room with Cadillac and chair.
Instructor academyTraining cohortsReformers in matching configurations plus Cadillac and barrel for teaching repertoire.
Rehab / wellness facility1–4 clients per sessionReformers with assisted work, trapeze table and upright rehab training units.

Defining the format first prevents the most common buying mistake: choosing attractive individual products without planning how the complete room will work. It also determines your teaching system, which can influence the models your instructors feel most confident teaching on.

If you are still deciding between formats, see how to start a Pilates studio for the full planning sequence from format to opening day.

2. Build the equipment mix around core training

Reformers are usually the starting point for a commercial studio because they support both group classes and private sessions. Chairs, Cadillacs, towers, barrels and spine correctors then extend the program according to floor area, budget and instructor skills.

ReformerCore class equipment for group and private training.Frame, carriage travel, spring system, rope, pulley, footbar positions, upholstery and capacity.
Wunda Chair / Pilates ChairCompact strength, balance and control work for studios with limited space.Handle positions, pedal stability, spring tensions, footprint and frame rigidity.
Cadillac / Trapeze TablePrivate instruction, assisted work and advanced training.Frame material, spring hooks, canopy stability, mat surface, accessories and setup space.
TowerWall or reformer-based spring training where floor efficiency matters.Hook layout, spring options, mounting style, stainless components and compatibility.

The goal is not to buy every category at once. It is to build a package the studio can operate well from the first day, then expand with revenue. For a deeper comparison of reformers specifically, read the professional reformer buying guide.

3. Work out how many machines the studio needs

Machine count is driven by class size and schedule turnover, not by how many machines fit in the room. A practical method for reformers:

  • Decide the maximum class size you will teach (for example 6 clients).
  • Plan one reformer per client in that largest class.
  • Add spare capacity for maintenance, spring changes and the occasional machine out of service — most studios plan roughly one spare reformer per 4–6 in use.
  • Estimate turnover: a 50-minute class with 10 minutes of reset time means each reformer is used about once per hour.

A studio running 6-client classes across a 10-hour teaching day therefore needs roughly 6–8 reformers to keep the schedule flowing. Over-buying reformers while under-buying Cadillacs and chairs is the most common mistake new owners make — the mix usually matters more than the reformer count.

4. Plan the room before choosing the models

Equipment is only part of the plan; the room has to work around it. Confirm these points before signing a lease or finalizing an order:

  • Reformer spacing: allow roughly 1.2–1.5 m between reformers for safe entry and exit during class, plus clear aisles to the door.
  • Ceiling height: check that Cadillac bars and tower springs have full range of motion — low ceilings rule out some equipment.
  • Floor loading: professional reformers weigh roughly 60–90 kg; confirm the floor can take the load before the machines arrive.
  • Access route: check door widths, corridors and elevators, because large machines move in as packed units.
  • Storage: allocate space for mats, props, spare springs and toolkits so the studio floor stays clean.
  • Power and lighting: plan outlets and lighting around the class layout before installation.

Sending a rough floor plan to your equipment supplier helps them flag spacing and layout problems before you order. A room measurement is often more useful than a general request for a "full set".

5. Set a realistic budget

Professional Pilates equipment prices vary widely depending on frame material, spring quality, upholstery and whether you buy through a reseller or direct from the factory. Instead of relying on a single number, understand the cost drivers:

  • Frame material: carbon steel, stainless steel and anodized aluminum differ in price, weight and corrosion resistance.
  • Spring systems: the spring set is the heart of a reformer; higher-grade springs with consistent tension cost more and last longer.
  • Upholstery: commercial-grade foam and vinyl hold up to daily studio use far better than home-grade materials.
  • Brand vs. factory-direct: buying from the manufacturer removes reseller margins, which is why most new studios and distributors start with factory-direct quotations.

Budget for more than the machines themselves:

  • Export packaging (wooden crate or protective carton) and packed dimensions.
  • International freight from the FOB port with your own forwarder.
  • Destination customs, import duties and local compliance checks.
  • Installation, spare parts and instructor training.
KCONE is an inquiry-based manufacturer — there is no public price list. Share your studio size, training format, target capacity and budget range, and the KCONE team will recommend an equipment list and quotation rather than a flat catalog price.

6. Evaluate the manufacturer, not only the product photo

A good Pilates equipment manufacturer makes it easy to understand how the equipment is built, packed, quoted and supported after delivery. Product photos are useful, but they are only one part of the decision.

  • Ask whether the supplier is selling a proprietary product line or only copying generic models.
  • Request detailed specifications for frame, springs, upholstery, dimensions, weight, colors and accessories.
  • Review product videos and close-up photos of moving parts, welds, pulleys, rails, padding and handles.
  • Confirm whether the supplier can support studio packages, distributor orders and repeat purchasing.
  • Clarify communication channels for quotation, production updates, spare parts and after-sales questions.
  • Ask which training use each model was designed for — similar-looking machines can feel very different in practice.
Compliance, import, medical and studio requirements vary by destination market. Buyers should confirm local requirements before ordering instead of assuming one document applies everywhere.

7. Prepare the right information for a quotation

For B2B Pilates equipment orders, a fast and accurate quotation depends on the buyer's context. Instead of asking only for a unit price, prepare the order brief first:

  • Product models and estimated quantities.
  • FOB Shanghai or DDU quotation preference.
  • Studio, distributor, chain, academy or wellness facility role.
  • Preferred color options and branding needs, if any.
  • Expected timeline and whether the project is urgent.
  • Questions about packaging, spare parts or product videos.

This keeps the discussion practical and helps the manufacturer recommend a more suitable equipment mix. A complete first message also makes the questions to ask a manufacturer easier to answer in one round.

8. Check export packaging and FOB handover early

Large Pilates machines are not small parcel products. Frames, upholstery, springs, accessories and moving parts need protective export packaging. KCONE quotes on FOB Shanghai or DDU terms; buyers arrange the main international freight with their own forwarder.

  • Ask for wooden crate or protective export packaging details.
  • Confirm whether the quotation is based on FOB Shanghai or DDU.
  • Check product dimensions and packed dimensions separately.
  • Confirm freight planning after port handover with your logistics partner.
  • Keep videos, photos and packing information for internal approval before payment.

Follow the FOB ordering checklist before requesting the quotation so nothing is missed in the handover.

9. Questions buyers should ask before ordering

  • Which models fit my class format and floor area?
  • What are the exact dimensions, weight and material specifications?
  • Which springs, handles, pulleys, ropes and accessories are included?
  • What color options are available as standard options?
  • What is the MOQ for this model and this destination?
  • What is the current lead time and production schedule?
  • How is each machine packed for export?
  • How can I review product photos, videos and details before confirmation?
  • What support is available for spare parts and after-sales questions?

10. Special programs and training systems

Buyers should confirm the current product and training arrangements with KCONE before including RX in a package, and the same applies to any specialized equipment with a defined training method.
